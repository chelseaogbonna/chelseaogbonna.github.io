Wearable Diabetic Foot Monitoring System

Embedded Sensor Integration & Kinematic Gait Analysis

01 Problem Statement

Diabetic foot ulcers (DFUs) are a major complication of diabetes, often caused by peripheral neuropathy and abnormal gait patterns that increase localized pressure on the foot. Because these changes develop gradually and may go unnoticed, early detection is essential for preventing ulcers, infection, and lower-limb amputation.

Conventional gait assessment methods have several limitations that make continuous, everyday monitoring difficult, preventing early intervention.

  1. Laboratory-based motion capture systems provide highly accurate measurements using optical cameras and reflective markers. However, these systems are expensive, require specialized facilities and trained personnel, and only capture a patient's gait during short clinical visits.
  2. Force plates and pressure-sensitive walkways are considered the gold standard for gait analysis, but they only collect data during a few walking steps in a controlled laboratory environment.
  3. Commercial smart insoles are very expensive. Since this project is intended for rural communities and low-resource settings, purchasing such equipment is often unrealistic.

These limitations highlight the need for a wearable system that is low-cost, portable, continous monitoring and non-invasive.

02 The Experiment

A wearable gait monitoring system was developed using two sensing modalities to capture both motion and force during walking.

The motion-based system consisted of

  • MPU6050 inertial measurement unit (IMU) This mapped out three-axis acceleration, angular velocity, and orientation (pitch and roll).
  • Force-sensitive resistors (FSRs) This measures plantar pressure and foot-ground contact throughout the gait cycle.
  • Hardware Hub: Used a Raspberry Pi Pico 2 to aggregate real-time sensor streams over custom low-latency Python acquisition scripts.

By comparing motion- and force-based sensing approaches, the project evaluates which sensor features most effectively identify gait abnormalities, demonstrating how embedded systems and wearable technology can support early intervention and improve diabetic foot care.

Walking trials were performed under five conditions to simulate both normal and abnormal gait patterns:

The following walking conditions were tested:

  • Normal Walking
  • Lean Left
  • Lean Right
  • Lean Forward
  • Lean Back

For analysis, the walking conditions were grouped into two categories:

  • Normal Gait
  • Incorrect Gait

Engineering Challenges

During initial testing, the breadboard was mounted directly on the foot. However, the USB cable connecting the microcontroller to the computer was too short, causing the connection to slip during walking trials and interrupt data collection.

Implemented Solutions

To improve reliability, the hardware configuration was redesigned by relocating the breadboard to the participant's shin, where it experienced less movement and reduced cable strain. This modification provided a more secure connection, minimized interruptions during data acquisition, and improved the consistency of the collected gait data.

03 Results

Pitch roll by condition (IMU Results)

We compared walking under different postures: normal, leaning back, leaning forward, and leaning left or right. Normal walking showed higher gyroscope values with more consistent patterns. Leaning back had lower gyroscope values and more spread, indicating less controlled motion. Leaning in other directions produced more variability and less consistent movement. Normal walking is smooth and efficient, while leaning makes movement less stable i.e. leaning back appears “stiff” and cautious, whereas other leans appear “messy” and unstable. This demonstrates that the IMU can clearly identify when walking posture is incorrect.

Feature Comparison Normal vs Abnormal Walking (IMU Results)

There is a huge difference between the Normal vs Abnormal walking therefore the IMU sensor can sense the differences between walking styles.

Peak Force (Force Sensor Results)

The typical maximum force applied during each step. In our measurements, normal walking shows a slightly higher and more variable peak force (median ~800) compared to incorrect walking (~790). This suggests that normal gait involves stronger, more confident heel strikes, whereas incorrect walking results in slightly softer and more controlled steps. However, the difference between normal and incorrect walking is small, so this feature alone is not a highly reliable indicator of gait abnormalities.

Summary of Findings: The results demonstrated that the IMU-based system was more effective than the force-based system at detecting abnormal gait patterns. Features derived from the IMU consistently distinguished normal and incorrect gait while the force-sensitive resistors (FSRs) measurements showed greater overlap between walking conditions. Overall, the findings suggest that a low-cost IMU-only wearable system provides an accurate, practical, and scalable solution for continuous gait monitoring, making it well suited for early diabetic foot ulcer risk assessment.

04 Discussion

The findings of this study indicate that IMU-based sensing provides a more comprehensive representation of gait mechanics than force-based sensing. While this study focused on controlled walking conditions, future work will expand testing to larger and more diverse participant populations, including individuals with diabetes and clinically diagnosed gait impairments, to evaluate the system under real-world conditions.

Additional studies will investigate walking across different terrains, footwear, walking speeds, and daily activities to improve model robustness. Future development will also incorporate machine learning algorithms that combine multiple IMU-derived features for real-time gait classification, personalized risk assessment, and early diabetic foot ulcer prediction.

Ultimately, integrating wireless communication, mobile health applications, and long-term remote monitoring could transform this system into an accessible tool for rehabilitation, fall detection, and preventative diabetic foot care.

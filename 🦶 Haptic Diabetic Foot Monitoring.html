Wearable Diabetic Foot Monitoring System

Embedded Sensor Integration & Kinematic Gait Analysis

01 Problem Statement

Diabetic foot ulcers (DFUs) are a major complication of diabetes, often caused by peripheral neuropathy and abnormal gait patterns that increase localized pressure on the foot. Because these changes develop gradually and may go unnoticed, early detection is essential for preventing ulcers, infection, and lower-limb amputation.

This project explores two low-cost wearable sensing systems for continuous gait monitoring: an inertial measurement unit (MPU6050) that measures acceleration, angular velocity, and body orientation, and force-sensitive resistors (FSRs) that capture plantar pressure distribution during walking.

By comparing motion- and force-based sensing approaches, the project evaluates which sensor features most effectively identify gait abnormalities, demonstrating how embedded systems and wearable technology can support early intervention and improve diabetic foot care.

02 The Experiment

A wearable gait monitoring system was developed using two sensing modalities to capture both motion and force during walking.

The motion-based system consisted of

  • MPU6050 inertial measurement unit (IMU) This mapped out three-axis acceleration, angular velocity, and orientation (pitch and roll).
  • Force-sensitive resistors (FSRs) This measures plantar pressure and foot-ground contact throughout the gait cycle.
  • Hardware Hub: Used a Raspberry Pi Pico 2 to aggregate real-time sensor streams over custom low-latency Python acquisition scripts.

Walking trials were performed under five conditions to simulate both normal and abnormal gait patterns:

The following walking conditions were tested:

  • Normal Walking
  • Lean Left
  • Lean Right
  • Lean Forward
  • Lean Back

For analysis, the walking conditions were grouped into two categories:

  • Normal Gait
  • Incorrect Gait

Engineering Challenges

During initial testing, the breadboard was mounted directly on the leg. However, the USB cable connecting the microcontroller to the computer was too short, causing the connection to slip during walking trials and interrupt data collection.

Implemented Solutions

To improve reliability, the hardware configuration was redesigned by relocating the breadboard to the participant's shin, where it experienced less movement and reduced cable strain. This modification provided a more secure connection, minimized interruptions during data acquisition, and improved the consistency of the collected gait data.

03 Results

Plantar Pressure Mapping (Kinetic Data)

The FSR force sensors successfully targeted local load variances across the heel, midfoot, and metatarsal zones throughout consecutive stance intervals.

Motion Signature Profiles (Kinematic Data)

The inertial array precisely isolated acceleration patterns during swing-to-strike changes, capturing crisp kinematic cadence marks.

Multimodal Data Fusion

By combining force mapping inputs side-by-side with spatial motion curves, the system successfully mapped out cohesive, clear representations of individual strides.

Summary of Findings: Synchronized data fusion proved that isolated point-tracking lacks the resolution needed for high-risk profiles. Merging crossflow pressure points alongside step trajectories provides a comprehensive view of walking symmetry that single-modality tracking systems miss.

04 Discussion

The project proves that combining in-shoe pressure maps with spatial motion tracking creates a powerful framework for continuous biomechanical screening. While clinical patient trials are the next step, the prototype delivers a high-fidelity foundation for passive ulcer-prevention tools.

Developing this platform advanced core competencies across hardware-software integration, real-time data streaming, signal processing loops, and experimental hardware testing.

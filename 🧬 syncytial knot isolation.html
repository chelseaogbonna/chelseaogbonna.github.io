The Problem

🩺 Background

Syncytial knots are multinucleated trophoblast structures naturally shed from the placenta throughout pregnancy. Their concentration and morphology have been associated with numerous pregnancy complications including preeclampsia, fetal growth restriction, hypoxia, and placental dysfunction.

Unfortunately, current isolation techniques suffer from several major limitations: 1. Manually picking the syncytial knots is labor intensive and highly operator dependent. 2. Using centrifugation methods introduces mechanical stress that can rupture syncytial knots and also centrifugation methods do not separate syncytial knots from other contaminants found in the sample. 3. Conventional membrane filtration frequently clogs due to placental debris and often traps desired particles alongside contaminants. These limitations reduce reproducibility while preventing large-scale biological analysis. Hence an automated microfluidic enrichment platform could provide a standardized method for isolating syncytial knots while maintaining structural integrity.

Why This Matters

🔬 Research

More efficient syncytial knot isolation enables researchers to better understand placental biology and investigate pregnancy-related diseases.

🏥 Clinical Impact

Improved isolation methods could contribute to future diagnostic tools for pregnancy complications including preeclampsia and fetal growth restriction.

⚙ Engineering Challenge

The device had to separate particles based primarily on size while maintaining continuous flow and minimizing clogging.

Design Requirements

🎯 Biological Constraints

  • Syncytial knots: 50–150 µm
  • Minimize cellular damage
  • Continuous sample flow
  • Preserve particle integrity

⚙ Engineering Constraints

  • 200 µm maximum channel height
  • 35 µm pillar spacing
  • Low pressure drop
  • Minimal clogging

🧪 Experimental Goals

  • High recovery efficiency
  • Reliable particle separation
  • Repeatable experiments
  • Easy fabrication

Design Evolution

The device was developed through multiple design iterations. Each version addressed limitations discovered during analysis and moved the project closer to a manufacturable and effective solution.

Engineering Analysis

Before fabrication, each design was evaluated using COMSOL Multiphysics to better understand fluid behavior, optimize geometry, and reduce the need for trial-and-error manufacturing.

Velocity Field

Simulations were used to visualize flow velocity throughout the device, ensuring smooth transport while minimizing stagnant regions where particles could accumulate.

Pressure Distribution

Pressure contours helped identify regions of elevated resistance and guided geometry modifications that improved overall flow performance.

Particle Trajectories

Particle tracing simulations predicted how different particle sizes would move through the crossflow device and informed design decisions before fabrication.

Experimental Validation

To evaluate device performance, multiple experiments were designed to assess particle separation efficiency, flow stability, cytotoxicity, and biological identification of isolated syncytial knots.

Recovery Efficiency

Fluorescent microparticles ranging from 30–150 μm were used to quantify particle recovery and verify successful size-based separation.

Planned Experiment

LDH Cytotoxicity Assay

An LDH assay will evaluate whether the microfluidic device preserves cell viability by measuring membrane damage after processing.

Planned Experiment

Immunofluorescence

DAPI and Cytokeratin-7 staining will confirm the biological identity of isolated syncytial knots following device separation.

Crossflow geometry reduced the likelihood of particle accumulation while maintaining continuous movement through the device.

🧬 Size-Based Separation

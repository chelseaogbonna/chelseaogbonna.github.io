User-Centered Recycling Condominium Redesign

Infrastructure Design, CAD Optimization & Human-Centered Engineering

01 Problem Statement & Criteria

Our site assessment revealed several barriers that prevented the condominium's recycling system from functioning effectively.

First, the recycling bins contained a significant amount of non-recyclable waste. According to the City of Chicago's recycling guidelines, entire truckloads can be rejected at the processing facility if their contamination threshold exceeds just 5% to 10% and sent to a landfill instead of being recycled. Based on our qualitative observations, interviews, and waste audits, we concluded that a large portion of the condominium's recyclable materials were likely being diverted to landfills due to contamination.

Second, the educational signage was ineffective. The instructions relied almost entirely on English text, assuming that all residents could read and interpret the information correctly. The signage also required residents to actively read through long lists of recyclable items before disposing of their waste, creating unnecessary cognitive effort. Additionally, some of the information was very vague, ie "NO Trash", which further contributes to improper waste sorting.

Finally, we analyzed the resident recycling journey and found that convenience strongly influenced disposal behavior. Throwing waste into the trash chute required only a short walk from each residential floor, whereas recycling required residents to collect their recyclables, take the elevator to the ground floor, walk through the garage, and locate the recycling room. This significantly longer and less convenient process discouraged participation and increased the likelihood that recyclable materials would instead be disposed of as general waste.

Collectively, these issues demonstrated that the existing recycling center was not simply an infrastructure problem, but a human-centered design problem. Our objective was to redesign the recycling experience to improve accessibility, reduce contamination, encourage resident participation, and create a more intuitive, efficient, and sustainable waste management system.

Core User Requirements

  • Improve general user accessibility thresholds
  • Maximize available site floor space and sorting pathways
  • Simplify sorting protocols to drastically reduce batch contamination
  • Maintain an aesthetically clean and welcoming appearance

Operational Requirements

  • Improve daily maintenance workflow loops
  • Increase bulk storage volume capacities
  • Create a completely modular, scalable architectural layout

02 The Iterative Design Process

We identified three main issues: lack of collection containers in individual units, the cumbersome transportation of recyclables, and unclear recycling guidelines.

To address these, we proposed a five-part system: the Recycling Rig, the Cardboard Caddy, updated recycling signage, location-specific posters, and repainting recycling room doors to reinforce recycling habits.

Design #1: Recycling Rig

Design 1 — Initial Concept

The initial concept focused on providing residents with a dedicated container for collecting recyclable materials inside their apartments.

Key Features
  • Open recycling container
  • Lightweight construction
  • Sized for apartment use
Why We Changed It
  • Did not utilize space efficiently
  • Was hard for single user condominium layout
  • User traffic flow became congested during peak hours

Design 2 — Improving Accessibility

The second iteration added portability features, making it easier for residents to transport sorting containers from individual apartment floors down to the central repository.

Improvements
  • Adjustable capacity for compact storage if needed
  • Lightweight wooden frame with 3D-printed joints
  • Waterproof canvas exterior to prevent leaks
What Still Needed Improvement
  • Visual sorting instructions inside the unit were still missing, leaving user error thresholds too high.

Design 3 — Modular Layout

The final Recycling Rig became a modular, collapsible recycling system capable of adapting to different household needs and explicitly isolating high-risk item batches.

Final Improvements
  • Adjustable capacity parameters
  • Fully modular partition system organization
  • Highly visible colored canvas sorting boundaries

Design #2: Cardboard Caddy

Design 1 — Initial Cardboard Concept

The initial concept proposed separating cardboard from mixed recyclables by introducing a dedicated collection area inside the recycling room.

Key Features
  • Single storage area for flattened cardboard
  • Stationary wall-anchored bracket
  • Reduced primary recycling bin volume overflow
Why We Changed It
  • A single storage compartment quickly became cluttered and overloaded.
  • Maintenance staff still faced vertical lifting issues trying to remove compressed heavy bundles.

Design 2 — Multi-Compartment Storage System

The second iteration introduced multiple distinct storage compartments sized for standard shipping and package box dimensions.

Improvements
  • Size-specific organizational tracking bays
  • Flexible nylon dividers to keep boxes upright
  • Low-friction acrylic sliding paths for easier loading
What Still Needed Improvement
  • The structure remained fixed to the floor, requiring maintenance teams to make manual, repetitive haul trips across the room.

Design 3 — Mobile Cardboard Caddy

The final Cardboard Caddy became a fully mobile collection station engineered to slide directly along building maintenance workflow lines.

Final Improvements
  • Heavy-duty directional lockable caster wheels
  • Dimensions structurally optimized to clear standard door frame widths
  • Drop-down side door panels for slide-out cleaning cycles
Final Outcome
  • Residents sort boxes by size seamlessly, while maintenance staff roll the unit out directly to garbage collection arrays without manual re-handling steps.

Design #3, 4, & 5: Updated Signage, Location Posters, & Door Painting

Design 1 — Information Overhaul

The updated recycling signage replaced text-heavy instructions with clear, image-based graphics that highlighted accepted recyclable materials. This simplified recycling guidelines, reduced confusion, and helped residents make more accurate disposal decisions.

Design 2 — Iconography & Visual Cues

The recycling room door was repainted using LRS's signature recycling blue to increase visibility and create a stronger visual association with the recycling area. The updated appearance encouraged residents to notice the redesigned recycling system and reinforced a consistent recycling identity throughout the building.

Design 3 — Spatial Environmental System

Location-specific posters were placed where recyclable items are commonly generated, such as the mailroom and laundry room, to provide timely reminders for residents. By tailoring the graphics to each location, the posters reinforced proper recycling habits at the point of disposal.

03 Final Optimized Architecture

The final design combined the strongest elements from every previous iteration into a fully optimized infrastructure solution. It merges the modular flexibility of Design 3 with the streamlined user corridors verified in Design 2.

Validated System Engineering Features:

  • Optimized Human Factors: Clear access points for users of all mobility levels with highly visible, anti-contamination sorting labels.
  • Simplified Operations: Hinged, front-loading panels allow maintenance staff to pull heavy bins directly out without vertical lifting strains.
  • Scalable Implementation: Standard structural panels enable quick adaptation to different residential footprint dimensions.

04 Project Results & Impact

The final recycling condominium design successfully satisfied all initial engineering requirements and stakeholder criteria. By evaluating physical and operational trade-offs across multiple design loops, the final solution maximizes space efficiency and simplifies maintenance workflows.

Engineering Recognition: This human-centered engineering system was awarded the Best Design Team Award, validating both our rigorous design process and the technical viability of the final solution.

Key Takeaway: This project highlights how iterative product development can solve real-world infrastructure challenges. Balancing user behaviors with manufacturing and spatial constraints results in practical solutions that promote long-term community participation and operational sustainability.
